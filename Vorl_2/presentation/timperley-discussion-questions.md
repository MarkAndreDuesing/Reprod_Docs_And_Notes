# Timperley et al. (2021): Discussion Questions, Abstract to §4 Results

Companion to `timperley-summary.md` (same headers). Background facts come from
`timperley-research.md`.

_Page numbers are the paper's "Page x of 41" = PDF pages._

**Format:**
- Each question has a **hook** (where in the paper it comes from) and **two opposing
  positions** (the quality test from `moderation.md`).
- **Count per unit:** 3 questions, or 5 where I marked a passage pink.
- **My own questions** (from my handwritten notes, reworded to stand alone) come on top,
  under "My questions", and don't count towards the 3 or 5.
- "Rec n" refers to the paper's recommendations in §6:
  - Rec 1 describe the artifact
  - Rec 2 make it self-contained
  - Rec 3 plan creation, sharing and maintenance
  - Rec 4 use a review rubric
  - Rec 5 align expectations
  - Rec 6 reduce reviewing cost
  - Rec 7 recognise and fund artifact work
  - Rec 8 long-term strategy

## §1 Introduction (3)

1. **Is "lack of community standards" a cause or a symptom?** The paper reads the
   controversy around Collberg's study as proof that no standards exist.
   - Hook: p. 2
   - A: standards are missing, so write them.
   - B: the controversy shows people *disagree* on what artifacts are for. More rules won't
     settle that.
2. **Are AECs really the right actor to fix artifact quality, or is this the wrong
   stage?** The authors say AECs are "well positioned".
   - Hook: p. 3
   - A: AECs are the only place where someone actually runs the artifact.
   - B: AECs come *after* acceptance and are optional, so quality has to be built in during
     the research (Rec 3), not checked at the end.
3. **The paper lists four root areas: communication, social system, time and technical
   aspects. Which one, if fixed, would solve the most?**
   - Hook: p. 3
   - A: the social system (rewards), because everything else follows from incentives.
   - B: technical/time, because even well-rewarded artifacts rot.

## §2.1 Software engineering artifacts (3)

1. **Should "artifact" mean anything linked from a paper, or only material that supports
   the results?** The paper counts companion sites and survey instruments.
   - Hook: p. 3
   - A: broad, because every shared item helps understanding.
   - B: narrow (ACM: created for or generated by the study), otherwise statistics on
     artifacts mean little.
2. **Does every paper need an artifact?** Respondents say "not every paper needs one"
   (p. 11), e.g. theoretical, purely qualitative or position papers.
   - A: yes, at least a data or replication package.
   - B: no, forcing it creates empty "ritual" artifacts.
3. **Which motivation should artifact sharing serve first: letting others replicate,
   reproduce, or build upon the work?**
   - Hook: p. 4
   - A: confirming results (scientific duty).
   - B: building upon (that's where most community value comes from).

## §2.2 Recognition of SE artifacts: AECs and badges (5)

Pink: the ACM quote "too early to establish more specific guidelines… may not be desirable
at all" (p. 5).

1. **Was the ACM right that more specific guidelines are "not desirable" given the
   diversity of communities?**
   - Hook: p. 5
   - A: yes. A proof, a dataset and a fuzzer can't share one rubric, so local rules
     (e.g. ICSE's) are better.
   - B: no. Without shared thresholds a badge means different things at every venue
     (Hermann et al.), and C6 is the result.
2. **Should artifact evaluation stay optional and post-acceptance, or move into paper
   review, as TACAS does (lecture slide 10)?**
   - Hook: p. 4, "papers cannot be rejected on the basis of their artifacts"
   - A: optional keeps the barrier low.
   - B: optional means the incentive is weak and few submit (26 %, p. 21).
3. **Do badges on the front page work as an incentive, or only as a signal to readers?**
   - Hook: p. 4
   - A: incentive. Visible recognition motivates authors.
   - B: badges don't count for hiring or promotion, so they are mostly decoration (links
     to C1/Rec 7).
4. **Is it a problem that ACM leaves the evaluation process entirely to each
   conference?**
   - Hook: p. 5
   - A: no, communities know their own artifacts best.
   - B: yes, "Reusable" at one venue ≠ "Reusable" at another, so the label misleads
     readers.
5. **ICSE now only awards Available, Functional and Reusable to its own papers, and the
   result badges only for older papers. Should conferences try to award "Results
   Reproduced" at all?**
   - Hook: p. 4 badge list; research item 1
   - A: yes, that's the badge that matters scientifically.
   - B: no, reproducing results is a separate study, too costly for an AEC within weeks.

**My questions**
- Should artifact evaluation allow formal exceptions, for example for industry papers
  whose data can't legally be shared? The ACM scheme already separates "works" from
  "public", and lets proprietary parts be replaced by proxies.
  - Background: research item 1

## §2.3 Diffusion of Innovations (3)

1. **Is it useful to treat each artifact as an "innovation" that must spread, or does that
   frame confuse research artifacts with products?**
   - Hook: p. 5
   - A: useful. Artifacts compete for adoption like tools do.
   - B: misleading. A replication package isn't meant to "spread", only to be checkable.
2. **Which DoI element do you think explains most of the problems: communication
   channels, time or the social system?**
   - Hook: pp. 5–6
   - A: communication (links, badges, AECs all transmit unclear information).
   - B: social system (no one is rewarded).
3. **Can a framework from the social sciences give SE researchers actionable insight, or
   does it mostly relabel survey findings?**
   - Hook: p. 5, "integrate findings… explain relationships"
   - A: it structures scattered findings.
   - B: it adds vocabulary, not evidence.

## §2.4 Implementation Science (3)

1. **Can medical "evidence-based improvement" methods be transferred to how research
   communities run artifact evaluation?**
   - Hook: p. 6
   - A: yes, iterate and measure policies like treatments.
   - B: no, conferences change chairs every year, so nobody runs the iterations.
2. **Who in the SE community would actually "trial, observe and improve" an
   artifact policy over many years?**
   - Hook: p. 6
   - A: steering committees or ACM (Rec 8).
   - B: nobody has that mandate today, so IS principles remain theory.
3. **Should research-community policies be held to the same evidence standard as the
   research itself?**
   - A: yes, otherwise we preach rigour but practise guesswork.
   - B: no, waiting for evidence delays obviously useful changes.

## §3 Methodology

### §3 Research questions and study design (3)

1. **Is RQ1 ("does the community perceive inherent value?") worth asking, when almost no
   researcher would say artifacts are worthless?**
   - Hook: p. 6
   - A: yes, it establishes a baseline.
   - B: no, value is obvious. The real question is why value doesn't lead to action.
2. **Is splitting RQ2 into creators, users and reviewers the right cut, given that most
   respondents are all three (Table 2)?**
   - Hook: p. 6
   - A: yes, roles bring different problems.
   - B: the same people answer each time, so the perspectives aren't independent.
3. **Does combining a publication survey with an author survey give a fuller picture
   than either alone?**
   - Hook: Fig. 1, p. 7
   - A: yes, behaviour plus reasons.
   - B: the two parts measure different populations and years, so they hardly connect.

### §3.1 Publication survey (5)

Pink: "identified 899 artifacts across 1434 papers… availability… by checking that its
URL could be accessed" (p. 7).

1. **Is "the URL can be accessed" a good enough test of availability?**
   - Hook: p. 7
   - A: yes, it's objective and scales to 1434 papers.
   - B: no. A live page with a dead download, or an unusable artifact, still counts (the
     authors admit this, p. 11).
2. **Should the authors have contacted paper authors for missing artifacts, as Collberg &
   Proebsting did?**
   - Hook: pp. 7, 10
   - A: yes, otherwise availability is underestimated.
   - B: no, a reader without contacts only has the paper, so that's the fair measure.
3. **Availability was checked once, in early 2019. How should we read a 26 % dead-link
   rate for 2014 vs. 5 % for 2018?**
   - Hook: Fig. 3
   - A: practice improved.
   - B: older links simply had more time to break (same issue as Heumüller et al.).
4. **Is a single snapshot enough, or should availability studies follow artifacts over
   several years?**
   - A: a snapshot is enough for prevalence.
   - B: only repeated checks show how fast artifacts decay.
5. **Would the results look different if "artifact" had meant runnable code, as in
   Heumüller et al.?**
   - Hook: p. 3 vs. week 1
   - A: yes, much lower.
   - B: the trend would be the same, only the level differs.

### §3.2 Author survey and qualitative analysis (5)

Pink: "a thematic analysis does not tell us how prevalent each of the experiences… might
be" (p. 9).

1. **If thematic analysis can't say how common a challenge is, how should a reader weigh
   C1–C12?**
   - Hook: p. 9
   - A: all are real and deserve attention.
   - B: without frequencies we can't prioritise, so the list is less useful for policy.
2. **Is a 22 % response rate "very strong"?**
   - Hook: p. 8
   - A: yes, typical SE surveys get less.
   - B: 78 % didn't answer, and those who did probably care more about artifacts
     (self-selection).
3. **Only 32 respondents (21 %) had reviewed artifacts. Is that enough to describe
   reviewer challenges?**
   - Hook: Table 2, p. 8
   - A: enough for themes (that's the goal).
   - B: too few, and Hermann et al. surveyed 257 reviewers.
4. **Should the survey also have reached researchers who have never shared an artifact,
   or who left academia?**
   - Hook: p. 7, recruitment from 2018 authors
   - A: yes, they hold the main reasons for *not* sharing.
   - B: they're hard to reach, and current authors are the ones a policy affects.
5. **Does mixing closed questions (Table 5 counts) with open coding risk presenting
   qualitative themes as if they were measured?**
   - Hook: p. 9 vs. p. 19
   - A: no, the paper keeps them apart.
   - B: yes. "First and third-most common" invites readers to rank everything.

**My questions**
- Is it worth explaining the coding terms (descriptive, axial, thematic), or does that
  detail distract from the findings?
  - This is a presentation choice, not a debate. I decide it myself.

### §3.3 Secondary analysis (3)

1. **Should a secondary analysis with a theoretical framework come before or after the
   primary findings are presented?**
   - A: after, as here, so the findings stand on their own.
   - B: before, so readers know what lens shaped the coding.
2. **Does integrating two studies with one framework risk forcing data to fit the
   framework?**
   - Hook: p. 9
   - A: no, DoI is only an organising tool.
   - B: yes, frameworks make every finding look like part of a neat system.
3. **Would a simpler cross-tabulation (challenge × perspective) give the same insight as
   DoI?**
   - A: largely yes.
   - B: no, DoI adds the time and communication dimensions.

### §3.4 Recommendations (5)

Pink: recommendations must not worsen known challenges, must be compatible, scalable,
sustainable, trialable and observable (p. 9), and "can be implemented, trialed, observed,
and evaluated" (p. 9).

1. **Can a recommendation be "trialable and observable" if nobody is assigned to trial
   and observe it?**
   - Hook: p. 9
   - A: yes, the criteria make trials *possible*.
   - B: no, without an owner (Rec 8) they stay untested proposals.
2. **"Does not exacerbate any known challenges": do Rec 1–Rec 3 (more documentation,
   self-contained artifacts, planning) add work for creators, worsening C1 "not worth
   it"?**
   - A: they save time later.
   - B: they shift even more unpaid work onto students.
3. **Which target group would change the most if it acted: creators, advisors, reviewers,
   organisers or community leaders?**
   - Hook: pp. 9–10
   - A: community leaders (hiring and funding rules, Rec 7).
   - B: advisors, who shape daily lab culture (Rec 3).
4. **Should the paper have evaluated at least one recommendation itself before
   publishing?**
   - A: yes, otherwise "evidence-based" is a claim.
   - B: no, a survey study's role is to propose and others test.
5. **Are recommendations "compatible with existing practices" ambitious enough, or does
   that criterion rule out the radical changes (e.g. mandatory artifacts) that some
   respondents want (p. 23)?**
   - A: compatibility makes adoption likely.
   - B: it protects the status quo.

**My questions**
- Given limited space, how much of the target-group detail is worth keeping?
  - This is a presentation choice, not a debate. I decide it myself.

### §3.5 Threats to validity (5)

Pink: the heading itself; "materials available… while preserving confidentiality";
artifacts not documented in the paper; internal validity; "available if the link is still
alive"; external validity (pp. 10–11).

1. **A study about artifact sharing can't share its qualitative data (confidentiality).
   Is that a contradiction, or the same legitimate constraint as C9?**
   - Hook: p. 10
   - A: legitimate, same as industry or privacy cases.
   - B: it shows the field accepts "can't share" too easily.
2. **The authors didn't contact authors "for practical reasons". Is practicality an
   acceptable reason in a study whose message is that artifact work should be worth the
   effort?**
   - Hook: p. 10
   - A: yes, scope has to be limited.
   - B: it mirrors C1, using the same excuse they criticise.
3. **Internal validity: respondents and researchers can be biased. Which bias do you think
   matters most here?**
   - Hook: p. 10
   - A: respondents presenting themselves as good sharers.
   - B: the authors (artifact advocates) reading the answers through their own views.
4. **"Available = link alive; quality is beyond our scope" (p. 11). Should a study on
   artifact *quality* (the abstract says "affect the quality of artifacts") measure
   quality at all?**
   - A: no, survey themes are enough.
   - B: yes, otherwise the quality claims rest only on opinions.
5. **Results only describe the SE research community (p. 11). Do you expect the same
   challenges in other fields, e.g. HCI, ML or physics?**
   - A: mostly yes, the incentives are the same.
   - B: no, fields with central data infrastructures (e.g. physics with CERN) differ.

**My questions**
- What does a "parallel-convergent mixed-methods design" actually add over running two
  separate studies? (Definition is in the summary.)
  - A: the integration step shows where the numbers and the opinions agree or contradict.
  - B: here the two parts barely overlap, so "integration" is mostly a label.

## §4.1 RQ1: Does the community perceive inherent value in artifacts? (5)

Pink: "almost two thirds (62.69 %)… contain an artifact" (p. 11).

1. **Does a high share of papers with links show that the community *values* artifacts, or
   only that linking became expected?**
   - Hook: p. 11
   - A: value. People share voluntarily.
   - B: norm. Reviewers expect a link (C9's "ritualistic blanket argument").
2. **62.69 % is far above Heumüller's 47.8 % linked. Which number better describes the
   state of SE?**
   - Hook: p. 11 vs. week 1
   - A: Heumüller, because runnable code is what matters.
   - B: Timperley, because data and survey material are artifacts too.
   - Background: research item 6
3. **"Supporting evidence" is a motivation for sharing (Table 3). Should an artifact be
   required whenever a paper makes empirical claims?**
   - A: yes, claims without evidence aren't checkable.
   - B: no, peer review of the paper should be enough for many claims.
4. **"Community norms": is "it's good practice" a strong enough reason to keep sharing
   when it costs time?**
   - Hook: Table 3
   - A: yes, norms drive most scientific behaviour.
   - B: no, norms erode without rewards (C1).
5. **Respondents want sharing "encouraged and rewarded". What reward would actually
   change your behaviour?**
   - Hook: p. 11
   - A: career credit (hiring, promotion, Rec 7).
   - B: direct benefit (citations, collaborations).

**My questions**
- Why is 62.69 % so much higher than the week-1 studies (Heumüller 47.8 % linked,
  27 % available; Collberg 32–54 % weakly repeatable)? Is it better practice, a broader
  definition of "artifact", or a later time period?
  - Background: research item 6

## §4.2 RQ2: Challenges, per challenge

### C1 Not worth it ■ (5)

Pink: the perceived risks (reputation, scooping, mistakes discovered, maintenance, no
users) and "untapped publication value… scooped… protect a Ph.D. student" (p. 13).

1. **Is "the time is better spent writing papers" a rational choice or a failure of the
   system?**
   - Hook: p. 13
   - A: rational, given how careers are judged.
   - B: a system failure that Rec 7 (counting artifacts in hiring) should fix.
2. **Is fear of being "scooped" a legitimate reason to delay sharing?**
   - Hook: p. 13
   - A: yes, especially for PhD students who need publications.
   - B: no, priority is established by the paper, and an embargo would be the better
     compromise.
3. **"Nobody else will use it" (limited audience). Should artifacts be shared even if
   probably nobody uses them?**
   - Hook: p. 13
   - A: yes, verifiability doesn't depend on users.
   - B: no, effort should go where there's demand.
4. **Would a citable artifact (own DOI, counted like a paper) remove the "not worth it"
   problem?**
   - Links to Rec 1 (citation guidelines) and Rec 7
   - A: yes, it turns artifacts into publications.
   - B: only if hiring committees actually count them.
5. **Respondent P68 says whole research areas died because early work had no artifacts.
   Does that collective cost outweigh the individual cost?**
   - Hook: p. 15
   - A: yes, science is a collective effort.
   - B: individuals can't be expected to carry costs that only the community benefits from.

**My questions**
- Researchers fear sharing artifacts because mistakes in their analysis might be found.
  But isn't finding mistakes exactly the point of scientific review?
- How much personal accountability for one's code and analysis should a researcher expect
  to carry once it's public?
- Does holding back artifacts to protect future publications slow down the progress of
  research as a whole?

### C2 Portability ■ ▲ ★ (5)

Pink (reviewers): VMs help portability and security "but are not without their own
problems"; huge, cross-OS issues, performance claims can't be assessed; "containers does
not always solve this problem" (pp. 21–22).

1. **Should authors be required to provide a VM or container image (Rec 2), or should
   clean installation instructions be enough?**
   - A: image required, it's the only reliable way.
   - B: instructions are enough, images hide how the environment was built.
2. **If a VM can't show realistic performance, should performance claims be excluded from
   artifact evaluation?**
   - Hook: P64, p. 22
   - A: yes, only evaluate functionality.
   - B: no, check relative trends (SPLASH 2026 asks for this).
3. **Respondents say containers don't stop software from degrading over time. Is that a
   problem with containers, or with how they are used?**
   - Hook: P18, p. 22
   - A: containers, because runtimes and architectures change too.
   - B: usage. A saved, pinned image avoids most rebuild problems.
4. **Running untrusted artifacts is a security risk for reviewers. Should reviewers be
   allowed to refuse artifacts that need root access or invasive libraries?**
   - Hook: pp. 18, 21
   - A: yes, safety first.
   - B: then whole kinds of system-level research become unevaluable.
5. **Rec 2 suggests testing installation automatically with CI. Is that a realistic
   expectation for prototype research code?**
   - A: yes, it's cheap once set up.
   - B: no, prototypes change too fast and CI adds maintenance.

**My questions**
- How can artifact reviewers accurately verify performance claims, if VMs distort timings
  and they rarely have the authors' hardware?
  - Background: research item 5
- One respondent complains that containers don't prevent software degradation. Is that
  really a container problem, or a problem of rebuilding from unpinned recipes instead of
  archiving the built image?
  - Background: research item 5

### C3 Maintenance ■ ▲ (5)

Pink: "ongoing investment… bugs… use cases evolve… inevitable bit rot" (p. 16).

1. **Should an artifact be expected to work only at publication time, or for a set
   period?**
   - A: publication time, the archive preserves the state.
   - B: a stated period, e.g. 5 years (Rec 3 maintenance plans).
2. **When a student graduates, who should own the artifact: the student, the advisor or
   the university?**
   - Hook: p. 16
   - A: the advisor or group (Rec 3 transition plans).
   - B: nobody. Archive it and mark it unmaintained.
3. **Users are "sympathetic" to unmaintained artifacts (p. 19). Should "unmaintained" be
   an accepted, honest state?**
   - A: yes, honesty beats false promises.
   - B: no, an artifact nobody can run doesn't serve its purpose.
4. **P20 says only well-funded groups maintain artifacts well. Does that create an
   unfair advantage in research?**
   - Hook: p. 19
   - A: yes, funding becomes a precondition for credibility.
   - B: no, archives make basic sharing free for everyone.
5. **Should funders pay for artifact maintenance after a project ends?**
   - Hook: p. 19, Rec 7
   - A: yes, otherwise investment is lost.
   - B: no, money should go to new research.

**My questions**
- Is software decay unavoidable as technology moves on? What practical ways are there to
  limit it (archived images, pinned dependencies, plain source code)?

### C4 Tacit knowledge ■ ▲ ★ (5)

Pink: creators, "highly automated way to utilize the artifact… knowledge buried in
developers' heads" (p. 16); reviewers, automation "not a panacea", black box, "replicate
the same mistake" (p. 22).

1. **Can tacit knowledge ever be fully written down, or will every artifact need its
   author at some point?**
   - Hook: p. 16
   - A: good documentation is enough.
   - B: some know-how only transfers by contact (which is why C12 matters).
2. **Is a one-click reproduction script good or bad for scientific scrutiny?**
   - Hook: p. 22
   - A: good, anyone can check results.
   - B: bad, it hides the process and re-runs the same mistakes.
3. **Should reviewers be required to look at the code, not just run it?**
   - Hook: p. 22, P6 on deep dives
   - A: yes, otherwise fraud and bugs pass.
   - B: no, that's a full code review, far beyond an AEC's time.
4. **Missing usage instructions are the most common user problem (103 of 130). Should a
   README be a hard requirement for publication?**
   - Hook: Table 5
   - A: yes, it costs little.
   - B: a requirement would produce minimal, box-ticking READMEs.
5. **Which matters more for reuse: documentation or code quality?**
   - Hook: p. 19
   - A: documentation, it tells you where to start.
   - B: code quality, documentation goes out of date.

**My questions**
- Tacit knowledge is an implicit human problem: knowledge stays in the developer's head.
  What are practical ways to tackle it (templates, screencasts, pair onboarding,
  automation)?
- How much effort, time and money should artifact reviewers be expected to invest before
  they may give up on an artifact?
- Automated scripts reproduce results, but can they help a reviewer understand *why* the
  results come out? If not, what can?

### C5 Artifact does not fit purpose ▲ (3)

1. **If an artifact only works for the paper's exact experiments, has it "failed"?**
   - Hook: p. 19
   - A: no, its purpose was to support the paper.
   - B: yes, if the paper claims general applicability.
2. **Should papers state the intended scope of their artifacts (replication only vs.
   reuse)?**
   - Links to Rec 1
   - A: yes, it aligns expectations.
   - B: authors would then claim narrow scope to avoid work.
3. **28 users had to reimplement an artifact. Is reimplementation a failure, or a
   valuable independent replication?**
   - Hook: Table 5
   - A: failure, wasted effort.
   - B: a gain, because independent re-implementation is the strongest form of
     confirmation (the 2020 meaning of *replicability*).

### C6 Lack of standards and guidelines ■ ★ (5)

Pink (reviewers): unclear purpose of review, running scripts vs. deep investigation; "if
the AEC doesn't do it, will anybody ever? Probably not"; review vs. shepherding, mandatory
artifacts vs. "rubber stamping" (pp. 22–23).

1. **What should artifact review be: running the authors' scripts, or investigating
   assumptions, limitations and generality?**
   - Hook: p. 22
   - A: running scripts, clear and feasible.
   - B: investigation, otherwise the badge certifies little.
2. **"If the AEC doesn't do a deep dive, will anybody ever?" Should AECs be the last line
   of defence for correctness?**
   - Hook: p. 23
   - A: yes, nobody else runs the code.
   - B: no, correctness is the paper reviewers' job, and AECs check usability.
3. **Should an improperly implemented benchmark found during artifact review lead to
   paper rejection?**
   - Hook: p. 23
   - A: yes, the claims don't hold.
   - B: no, it would make authors avoid submitting artifacts.
4. **Should artifacts be mandatory for paper acceptance (P124, P146)?**
   - Hook: p. 23
   - A: yes, unless legal reasons prevent it.
   - B: no, it would push some research out of top venues.
5. **Should creators and reviewers get the same written rubric (Rec 4, Rec 5)?**
   - A: yes, it removes surprises.
   - B: a rubric invites authors to optimise for the checklist instead of quality.

**My questions**
- Isn't setting guidelines exactly what an AEC is supposed to do? If you could rewrite the
  ACM badge criteria or an AEC's call, what would you change?
- What is the point of artifact review if it becomes a quick "rubber stamp" for any linked
  repository?

### C7 Hosting ■ ▲ (5)

Pink: P12, "a large dataset that does not fit into a GitHub repo or Dropbox… not intended
as a long-term archive" (p. 17); Fig. 3, availability over time (p. 20).

1. **Should only archival repositories count for linking artifacts in papers, not GitHub
   or personal pages?**
   - Hook: p. 17; ICSE now requires an archive for the Available badge
   - A: yes, links must last.
   - B: no, GitHub shows the living version and history (Rec 3 suggests both).
2. **Who should pay for hosting large research data: authors, universities, publishers
   or public infrastructure?**
   - Hook: p. 17
   - A: public infrastructure (like CERN's Zenodo).
   - B: universities, as part of research costs.
3. **Is an archived but unrunnable artifact still "available"?**
   - Hook: Fig. 3 counts live links only
   - A: yes, the code can still be read.
   - B: no, availability without usability is symbolic.
4. **Zenodo promises retention for "the lifetime of the repository", tied to CERN. Is that
   long enough for science?**
   - A: yes, longer than most funder requirements (10–20 years).
   - B: no, scientific records should outlive any single institution.
5. **Should conferences re-check artifact links a few years after publication and mark
   dead ones?**
   - Hook: Fig. 3
   - A: yes, it keeps badges honest.
   - B: no, who would pay for it, and what would follow?

**My questions**
- Isn't hosting a solved problem with Zenodo? Free hosting, a DOI per version, 50–200 GB,
  automatic GitHub archiving. What remains unsolved?
  - Background: research item 2
- What would you do if your artifact were several terabytes, too big for Zenodo and for a
  reviewer's laptop?
  - Background: research item 3
- How do the availability numbers in this paper compare with the two week-1 studies, and
  what do the differences tell us?
  - Background: research item 6

### C8 Double-blind review ■ (3)

1. **Is double-blind review worth the extra effort it causes for sharing artifacts?**
   - Hook: p. 17
   - A: yes, it reduces bias in paper review.
   - B: no, for artifacts it mainly causes hassle and less sharing at submission.
2. **Should artifacts be submitted with the paper (anonymised) or only after acceptance
   (non-anonymous)?**
   - A: with the paper, so reviewers can check claims.
   - B: after acceptance, which avoids anonymisation work.
3. **Are temporary anonymous mirrors (e.g. Anonymous GitHub) a good solution, given that
   their links expire after review?**
   - A: yes, review only needs short-term access.
   - B: no, authors then have to manage two copies, and links in old reviews die.

### C9 External constraints ■ ▲ ★ (5)

Pink: P22, "Artifact should not be used as ritualistic blanket argument to judge papers"
(pp. 17–18); users, licensing, decompiling, paid licences (p. 20); reviewers, missing
licences, proprietary components (p. 23).

1. **Should paper reviewers be allowed to criticise a paper for not sharing data that its
   industry partner won't release?**
   - Hook: P22, pp. 17–18
   - A: yes, unshared data weakens the evidence.
   - B: no, that punishes industry collaboration and is a "ritualistic" argument.
2. **Is proxy data (as the ACM "Complete" criterion allows) a real substitute for
   withheld data?**
   - A: yes, it shows the analysis works.
   - B: no, it can't confirm the actual results.
3. **Should research tools that depend on paid commercial software be accepted as
   artifacts at all?**
   - Hook: p. 20
   - A: yes, much real-world SE uses commercial tools.
   - B: no, others can't use or check them.
4. **De-identified qualitative data can still be re-identified (p. 18). Should
   qualitative data ever be shared?**
   - A: yes, carefully anonymised and under access control.
   - B: no, participants' privacy comes first.
5. **P35 recovered source code with a decompiler to extend an artifact. Is that
   acceptable research practice?**
   - Hook: p. 20
   - A: yes, if it's the only way to build on the work.
   - B: no, it breaks licences and the result can't be shared anyway.

**My questions**
- Should there be formal exceptions to artifact evaluation for cases like industry data,
  following the ACM approach of separating "evaluated" from "publicly available"?
  - Background: research item 1
- How open source should research software be, and how feasible is full openness given
  IP, privacy and commercial tools?
  - Background: research item 8, Horizon Europe "as open as possible, as closed as
    necessary"
- If a reviewer lacks a licence for a proprietary component, whose responsibility is it?
  Should reviewers be expected to pay, or is automatically rejecting such an artifact
  fair? (The ACM policy is silent on this; each AEC decides.)

### C10 Lack of reviewer incentives ★ (5)

Pink: "gets students involved… but reinforces the idea that artifacts are not a primary
thing… inconsistent and low-quality reviews" (p. 23).

1. **Is letting students do artifact evaluation a good training opportunity or a sign
   that artifacts aren't taken seriously?**
   - Hook: P64, p. 23
   - A: training, students learn what good artifacts look like.
   - B: devaluation, and inexperience lowers review quality.
2. **Should artifact reviewing count the same as paper reviewing, e.g. for programme
   committee membership?**
   - A: yes, the effort is comparable.
   - B: no, judging scientific contribution needs more expertise.
3. **Would a "best artifact reviewer" award (Rec 6) change anything?**
   - A: yes, cheap recognition helps.
   - B: no, awards don't make up for days of unpaid work.
4. **Should artifact reviewers be paid, or given compute and travel support?**
   - A: yes, it professionalises evaluation.
   - B: no, peer review is unpaid everywhere, so why differ?
5. **Inconsistent reviews: should every artifact get two reviewers, or only a second one
   when problems arise (Rec 6's "lazy second evaluation")?**
   - A: always two, for fairness.
   - B: lazy second, which saves scarce reviewer time.

### C11 Technical obstacles ★ (5)

Pink: long execution times, compute, "more time than one can devote… (~ one day)", disk
space (p. 23); "practically infeasible to replicate… cloud-based contributions, fuzzers,
automated program repair" (pp. 23–24).

1. **Is it fair to have a fixed time limit (e.g. one day, or ICSE's 30-minute setup) after
   which an artifact fails?**
   - Hook: p. 23
   - A: yes, it forces authors to make artifacts usable.
   - B: no, it disadvantages inherently heavy research.
2. **If some experiments (fuzzing, program repair, cloud systems) can't be re-run by
   reviewers, should their papers get weaker badges, or a different kind of evaluation?**
   - Hook: pp. 23–24
   - A: weaker badges, be honest about what was checked.
   - B: different evaluation, e.g. reduced experiments or author-provided remote access
     (MobiSys, IISWC).
3. **Should conferences or ACM/IEEE provide compute for artifact reviewers (Rec 7)?**
   - A: yes, otherwise resource-rich labs are favoured.
   - B: credits were tried (SOSP 2019) and proved hard to use, so authors should provide
     access.
4. **Is a "reduced but representative" run (Rec 2) a valid check of a paper's claims?**
   - A: yes, it shows the method works.
   - B: no, effects that only show at scale aren't checked.
5. **If results can't be reproduced in practice, should the paper still be published in a
   top venue?**
   - Hook: pp. 23–24
   - A: yes, otherwise important large-scale research is excluded.
   - B: no, unverifiable results damage trust.

**My questions**
- Should compute resources be provided to reviewers? Would remote servers combined with
  containers solve the compute, disk-space and consistency problems, and is that
  realistic?
  - Background: research item 4
- Reviewers struggle when a VM image doesn't fit on their disk. What happens to artifacts
  bigger than an ordinary PC's storage? Are they simply unreviewable under the current
  process?
  - Background: research item 3
- If whole classes of experiments can't be reproduced, how damaging is that to the
  scientific process?

### C12 Limited communication ★ (5)

Pink: "a minor glitch in the installation instructions should not be grounds for
rejection… owners… willing to help the users resolve outstanding issues" (p. 24).

1. **Should artifact evaluation be a one-shot review or an iterative dialogue with
   authors?**
   - Hook: p. 24
   - A: iterative, glitches are inevitable.
   - B: one-shot, since later users won't have the author's help either.
2. **If an artifact only works with help from its authors, does it deserve a badge?**
   - A: yes, the review fixes the problems for later users.
   - B: no, the badge should certify that it works *without* the authors.
3. **Should authors have a deadline to answer reviewer questions, e.g. ICSE 2026's
   72 hours?**
   - Hook: p. 24, unresponsive authors
   - A: yes, it protects reviewers' time.
   - B: it's too strict for authors with teaching or travel.
4. **How can reviewers stay anonymous while talking to authors (Rec 6's anonymising
   mailbox, HotCRP messages)? Does anonymity matter for artifact review?**
   - A: yes, to avoid pressure.
   - B: no, artifact review is technical, and open dialogue is faster.
5. **Should artifacts ship with a contact point and a stated support period (Rec 3)?**
   - A: yes, users know what to expect.
   - B: no, promises of support are rarely kept.

**My questions**
- Respondents value authors who help users fix problems. Is that sustainable, given that
  support can't be offered forever?
