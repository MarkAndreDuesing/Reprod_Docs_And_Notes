## General summary (Abstract)

- **Why artifacts matter:** tools, benchmarks and data support the dissemination of ideas,
  provide evidence for research claims and serve as a starting point for future research.
- **Problem:** in practice, artifacts suffer from a variety of issues that keep them from
  reaching their full potential.
- **Goal:** understand the challenges of **creating, sharing and using** artifacts.
- **Method:** a mixed-methods study:
  - a survey of artifacts in SE publications
  - an online survey of 153 SE researchers
  - analysed from three perspectives: **creators, users and reviewers**
- **Findings:**
  - several high-level challenges that affect artifact quality
  - among them, **mismatched expectations** between the three groups and a **lack of
    sufficient reward** for creators and reviewers
- **Analysis:** Diffusion of Innovations (DoI) is used to examine how the challenges relate
  to one another and which factors affect the sharing and success of artifacts.
- **Output:** recommendations to improve artifact quality.

## Definitions

- **Artifact (general, §1, p. 2):** tools, benchmarks, data and more.
  - **Tools:** tangible, concrete implementations of abstract concepts that can be shared,
    studied and tested.
  - **Benchmarks:** the means to evaluate and compare implementations; a yardstick for
    progress in a field.
  - **Datasets** (and scripts): used to run experiments, test hypotheses and uncover new
    insights.
  - Since ideas can't be tested quantitatively, all claims are ultimately made with respect
    to these concrete (imperfect) artifacts.
- **Artifact (this paper's definition, §2.1, pp. 3–4):** *any external material or
  information provided with a research paper via a link*, i.e. anything authors develop and
  link to from the paper.
  - Examples: replication packages, tools/source code, companion sites, benchmarks, raw
    data, curated datasets, survey instruments and results, mechanised proofs.
  - **ACM definition:** "a digital object that was either created by the authors to be used
    as part of the study or generated by the experiment itself", e.g. software systems,
    experiment scripts, input datasets, raw data, analysis scripts.
  - **Motivations for sharing:** to let others **replicate, reproduce or build upon**
    existing work.
- **AEC, Artifact Evaluation Committee (§2.2, p. 4):** the committee that formally evaluates
  the artifacts of accepted papers.
  - First AEC at FSE 2011; ICSE held its first in 2019.
  - Evaluation is optional and opt-in after paper acceptance; a paper can't be rejected
    because of its artifact.
- **ACM badges (§2.2, p. 4):**
  - three badges: **Artifacts Available**, **Artifacts Evaluated**, **Results Validated**
  - FSE and ICSE participate since July 2020
  - badges appear on the paper's front page and in the ACM Digital Library metadata
  - after acceptance, a paper may receive **Evaluated: Functional** or **Evaluated:
    Reusable**, depending on quality and potential for reuse
  - introduced at several SE venues as an *optional* formal review process. It lets the
    claims of artifacts be assessed, uncovers usability issues others might run into, and
    signals artifact quality to the community. Its implementation has met both praise and
    criticism (§1, p. 2).
- **ACM artifact quality criteria (pp. 4–5):** quality = the extent to which an artifact is:
  - **Documented:** at minimum an inventory of the artifacts and enough description to
    exercise them.
  - **Consistent:** relevant to the paper and contributes to generating its main results.
  - **Complete:** all components relevant to the paper are included, as far as possible.
  - **Exercisable:** the scripts/software that generate the results can be executed
    successfully, and the included data can be accessed and manipulated.
- **Diffusion of Innovations, DoI (§2.3, pp. 5–6):** a social-science framework that
  explains how new objects, ideas and practices spread: how and why ideas take hold quickly,
  or are briefly acknowledged and then fade.
  - In this paper: an analytical framework to integrate the findings of the different study
    parts, explain their relationships and build a fuller understanding of artifacts.
  - Its four central elements as applied here:
    - **Innovation:** each individual artifact
    - **Communication channels:** artifact links, AECs and badges between creators, users and
      reviewers
    - **Time:** factors affecting artifact availability and usability over time
    - **Social system:** the SE research community, with its norms, practices, members and
      values
- **Implementation Science, IS (§2.4, p. 6):** studies the factors that effectively advance
  research and move it into practice.
  - It works through iterative processes: effective practices and policies are identified,
    trialled, observed and improved over many studies.
  - Researchers in many fields want results widely available, built upon and quickly
    applied in practice; IS principles help ideas diffuse.
- **Descriptive coding (§3.2, pp. 8–9):** each response segment is assigned basic labels to
  build an inventory of codes.
- **Axial coding (p. 9):** organises the codes, decides which themes dominate, and finds
  themes that offer opportunities to improve policy, process or practice.
- **Thematic analysis (p. 9):** finds the full range of themes. It does **not** show how
  prevalent each theme is, only that it exists in the community.
- **Secondary analysis (§3.3, p. 9):** the two primary studies (publications, authors) give
  results that place artifacts in the wider SE community. A secondary analysis with DoI as
  the analytical tool contextualises and integrates these results and examines
  relationships between findings.
- **Parallel-convergent mixed-methods design (§3.5, p. 10):** quantitative data (here: the
  publication survey) and qualitative data (here: the author survey) are collected **at the
  same time**, analysed **separately**, then **merged** in one integrated analysis to see
  where they agree or differ (triangulation).

## §1 Introduction (points only made here)

Everything else from the Introduction is merged into the sections below.

- For an artifact to be usable by others it must be **complete, structured and well
  documented**, which takes significant effort from authors (p. 2).
- Earlier work (Collberg et al. 2015; Collberg & Proebsting 2016) shows that many artifacts
  have issues such as missing documentation and unstated dependencies. These issues prevent
  reuse, extension and replication. The **controversy** around that work suggests there are
  **no clear community standards** for creating and evaluating artifacts (p. 2).
- The issues arise from four areas (p. 3):
  - inadequate **communication**
  - **social systems** (e.g. empowerment and reward)
  - effects of **time** (e.g. "bitrot")
  - **technical aspects** of the artifacts themselves (e.g. ease of use)
- The authors argue that **AECs are well positioned** to tackle many of the identified
  issues and to raise and assure artifact quality (p. 3).

## §3 Methodology

### Research questions (p. 6)
- Goal: insight into the values, norms and practices of the SE research community, and the
  reasons for and extent to which artifacts are created, shared and used.
- **RQ1:** Does the SE research community perceive inherent value in artifacts?
- **RQ2:** What are the challenges of creating, sharing, using and reviewing artifacts? It
  is split by perspective:
  - **RQ2.1** challenges for artifact **creators**
  - **RQ2.2** challenges for artifact **users**
  - **RQ2.3** challenges for artifact **reviewers**

### Study design (Fig. 1, p. 7; contributions list, p. 3)
- **Data collection:**
  - publication survey (AEC submission data, publication artifacts)
  - author survey (online questionnaire)
- **Primary analysis:**
  - artifact prevalence and availability
  - quantitative and qualitative analysis of the survey (axial coding, thematic analysis)
  - integrated results in §4
- **Secondary analysis:** DoI (innovations, social system, time, communication) relates
  the challenges to each other (§5).
- **Recommendations:** derived using IS, for creators, advisors and mentors, reviewers,
  process organisers and community leaders (§6).
- Replication package on Zenodo: survey materials, quantitative results, publication
  survey, mining scripts and graphs (p. 3).

### §3.1 Publication survey (p. 7)
- All technical-track papers from 2014–2018 at ICSE, FSE, ASE and EMSE.
- URLs were extracted from the paper PDFs and checked manually. Result: **899 artifacts in
  1434 papers**.
- **Availability** = the artifact's URL could be accessed when checked manually (29 Jan –
  6 Feb 2019).
- The AEC organisers of FSE 2015–2018 were asked how many papers were submitted to the AEC
  and how many were accepted.

### §3.2 Author survey (pp. 7–8)
- Online questionnaire on the perceived value and the challenges of creating, sharing,
  using and reviewing artifacts.
- **28 questions**, closed and open. **Branching logic** decides which subpopulation a
  respondent belongs to (creator, user, reviewer) and shows only the relevant questions.
- **Sample:** authors of at least one technical-track paper at ICSE, FSE, ASE or EMSE
  in 2018. Of 744 emails, 46 bounced, and **153 of the remaining 698 completed the survey
  (22 %)**.
- **Respondents (Table 2):**
  - roles: 139 academic researchers (91 %), 8 industrial researchers (5 %), 6 industrial
    practitioners (4 %)
  - subpopulations (overlapping): creators 137 (90 %), users 129 (84 %), reviewers 32 (21 %)
- **Survey questions by research question (Table 1):**
  - **RQ1 (value):**
    - Q5 why you shared the artifact of your most recent paper
    - Q10 why you used someone else's artifact
    - Q12 what an artifact should have, be or do
  - **RQ2.1 (creators):**
    - Q3 ever published an artifact
    - Q4 why publishing an artifact was not the right choice
    - Q6 artifact shared with your last three papers
    - Q7 reasons for not sharing
    - Q8 difficulties when creating artifacts
    - Q9 ever submitted to an AEC
  - **RQ2.2 (users):** Q11 challenges using others' artifacts
  - **RQ2.3 (reviewers):**
    - Q13 ever served on an AEC
    - Q14 up to three problems with the AE process
    - Q15 up to three improvements
  - **All RQs:** Q16 other positive or negative experiences
  - **Demographics:** Q1 role, Q2 number of publications
- **Analysis:** descriptive, then axial coding (see Definitions) by three domain experts and
  one methodologist.

### §3.3 Secondary analysis (p. 9)
- Defined under Definitions; applied in §5, outside my section.

### §3.4 Recommendations (pp. 9–10)
- Recommendations address specific challenges and follow IS principles.
- Each one is checked so that it doesn't make any known challenge worse, is compatible with
  existing practice, and is likely to be scalable, sustainable, trialable and observable.
- Each can be implemented, trialled, observed and evaluated for whether it achieved its
  goal.
- They are tailored to subgroups:
  - **Creators:** primary researchers (often students and postdocs, who write the code and
    maintain artifacts) and their mentors and advisors (less technical, but able to promote
    and support artifact work)
  - **Reviewers:** AEC members, journal reviewers, programme committee members
  - **Process organisers:** AEC chairs, conference chairs, journal editors
  - **Community leaders:** steering committees, editors, professional organisations,
    hiring and promotion committees, funding agencies

### §3.5 Threats to validity (pp. 10–11)
- **Replicability:** qualitative studies are hard to replicate. The authors share as much
  as possible while protecting respondents' confidentiality (publication data, survey
  design materials, de-identified quantitative answers, a Jupyter notebook for the
  figures).
- **Construct:**
  - An artifact = a paper links to accompanying material.
  - Papers may have artifacts that aren't mentioned in the paper and could only be found by
    searching or contacting authors. Authors were not contacted, for practical reasons.
  - Mitigation: a parallel-convergent mixed-methods design (see Definitions) combining
    publication data, survey data and an integrated analysis.
- **Internal:**
  - Surveys can be biased, intentionally or not, by respondents and by researchers.
  - Mitigations: respondents report their own experiences; collaborative, documented
    analysis; purposeful sampling; triangulation of researchers and analyses.
  - **"Available" only means the link is alive.** The content could be unusable or the
    download gone; artifact quality is out of scope.
- **External:**
  - Survey results can't be generalised beyond the survey population.
  - Mitigation: authors sampled from several top SE conferences and a leading journal.
  - Results describe only the SE research community.

## §4.1 RQ1: Does the SE research community perceive inherent value in artifacts?

- **Prevalence (Fig. 2, pp. 11–12):** **62.69 %** of papers at ASE, EMSE, FSE and ICSE
  (2014–2018) contain an artifact, rising from 50.56 % (2014) to 69.47 % (2018).
- **Perceived value:**
  - The community does perceive inherent value in artifacts.
  - Not every paper needs one, but respondents are positive about sharing and believe it
    should be **encouraged and rewarded**.
- Artifacts allow meaningful **analysis and evaluation** of an approach. **Replication** is
  very difficult, and often unreliable, without the information they contain.
- **Motivations for sharing (Table 3, p. 12):**
  - **Supporting evidence:** for the paper's claims, raising readers' and reviewers'
    confidence
  - **Community norms:** a commendable practice that benefits the community
  - **Facilitate reuse:** tools and datasets that others can reuse and extend
  - **Improving understanding:** additional information not suited to a paper (technical
    details, raw and preprocessed results)
## §4.2 RQ2: Challenges of creating, sharing, using and reviewing artifacts

**Context for the reviewer perspective (Fig. 4, p. 21):**
- Relatively few papers with artifacts are submitted for evaluation, even where an AEC
  exists. At FSE 2015–2018: 281 papers, 196 with an artifact, **74 submitted (26 %)**,
  **64 accepted (86 %)**.
- The reviewer challenges help explain this low participation.
- Some reviewers are happy with the current form of AECs.

### C1 Not worth it ■
- Creating and sharing artifacts is **difficult and time-consuming** and does **little for
  one's career**. The time is better spent on higher-value activities such as paper writing,
  so there is a high **opportunity cost** (p. 13).
- **Risks outweigh the rewards** (p. 13):
  - poor code quality could harm one's **reputation**
  - fear of being **"scooped"**
  - fear of **mistakes in the analysis being discovered**
  - the **burden of maintaining** the artifact
  - the artifact may be **used by no one**
- Some are reluctant to share artifacts with **untapped publication value**, e.g. to
  protect a PhD student's ability to produce enough results first (p. 13).
- Belief in a **limited audience**: few others work on the topic (p. 13).
- **Counterpoint:** sharing matters especially in emerging areas. "Whole research areas are
  dead" because early work had no artifacts and re-implementing it to move on was too
  costly (p. 15).

### C2 Portability ■ ▲ ★
- **[creator]** It is hard to **anticipate** all environments an artifact may be used in
  (p. 15).
- **[creator]** Fixing this afterwards may need extensive **refactoring** or a **VM or
  container image**. The repackaged artifact should be tested in several environments, and
  VM images bring their own problems, e.g. size (p. 15).
- **[creator]** Time pressure prevents documenting everything needed to run or use the
  artifact (p. 15).
- **[user]** Hard-wired, undocumented dependencies on files and folders such as the
  author's home directory; some artifacts need **root** access (p. 18).
- **[user]** From Table 5 (p. 19, 130 answers):
  - couldn't build due to unavailable software, library or compiler: 69
  - couldn't run due to a missing package or tool: 66
  - had to modify the source code: 60
  - run-time error: 55
  - had to reimplement the artifact: 28
- **[reviewer]** **Installing** artifacts and dependencies is a major challenge: overly
  complicated setup, complex and invasive system libraries (p. 21).
- **[reviewer]** **VMs** reduce portability problems and the security risk of running
  untrusted code on one's own machine (pp. 21–22), but:
  - they cause cross-OS problems and are huge
  - **performance claims can't be assessed accurately** in a VM, and authors shouldn't be
    penalised for that
- **[reviewer]** **Containers** help with performance and size. But software **degrades
  over time**: what compiles today probably won't in five years, and containers don't always
  solve this (p. 22).

### C3 Maintenance ■ ▲
- **[creator]** Sharing needs an upfront investment and an **ongoing one**: bugs are found,
  use cases evolve and **bitrot** sets in, so artifacts need updates (p. 16).
- **[creator]** **Students** usually create, share and maintain artifacts, which is hard to
  continue once they leave (p. 16).
- **[user]** Unmaintained artifacts rely on **old library versions** that are hard to find
  and get harder to make work (p. 19).

### C4 Tacit knowledge ■ ▲ ★
- **[creator]** Writing documentation and refactoring code is challenging and burdensome
  (p. 16).
- **[creator]** Tacit knowledge remains a major obstacle (p. 16):
  - knowledge is "buried in developers' heads"
  - it is hard to provide a highly **automated** way to use the artifact
  - the problem is compounded by the fast-changing, **prototypical** nature of most
    artifacts
- **[user]** Artifacts are hard to understand, build and use because of **missing
  documentation and poor code quality**. Guidance is needed, and that takes time (p. 19).
  In Table 5, missing *usage* instructions are the most common problem (103) and missing
  *build* instructions the third most common (79).
- **[reviewer]** Some couldn't reproduce the paper's analysis from the instructions alone
  and had to read the paper to find out what was missing (p. 22).
- **[reviewer]** **Automation** (scripts that replicate the results) is suggested as a
  remedy, but it is **not a panacea** (p. 22):
  - if results don't match, it is hard to tell whether the artifact is faulty or the
    reviewer is using it wrongly
  - a script can act as a **black box**: it produces the expected results without showing
    whether the process behind them is sound
  - automation can stop **methodological errors** from being noticed, because AECs asking
    for scripts are likely to replicate the authors' mistakes
  - documentation is still needed for a thorough evaluation, e.g. to know where to add new
    case studies

### C5 Artifact doesn't fit purpose ▲
- **[user]** Artifacts can be hard to use for the purpose stated in the paper: they "don't
  really serve the purpose stated in papers" and work only for very limited use cases
  (p. 19).
- Using them may need major changes, up to modifying or reimplementing them (counts
  under C2).

### C6 Lack of standards and guidelines ■ ★
- **[creator]** No clear standards or expectations for the **packaging, contents and
  quality** of artifacts (p. 16).
- **[creator]** Unclear how to handle artifacts that build on **public datasets or existing
  artifacts**: create a new artifact or point to the existing one? (p. 16)
- **[creator]** The community lacks guidelines on what must be included to make artifacts
  usable and reproducible. Setup instructions, contribution guides and sufficient
  documentation are often overlooked (p. 16).
- **[reviewer]** Guidelines for reviewing are **ambiguous or missing**, and there are no
  criteria for a good artifact. The evaluation scheme is hard to understand and its criteria
  hard to apply (p. 22).
- **[reviewer]** **Badge criteria** are unclear too. The ACM description is "very
  confusing" for reviewers and authors, and too general to decide which badge to give
  (p. 22).
- **[reviewer]** The **purpose of artifact review** is unclear, and reviewers disagree on
  whether to just run the scripts or investigate assumptions, limitations and generality
  in depth (pp. 22–23):
  - deep dives find severe problems but take days, yet "if the AEC doesn't do it, will
    anybody ever?"
- **[reviewer]** The relationship between **artifact review and paper shepherding** is
  unclear (p. 23):
  - an improperly implemented benchmark "should be grounds for paper rejection"
  - views range from evaluating artifacts as part of the paper, or making them mandatory
    for acceptance, to making artifact review a lightweight "rubber stamp"

### C7 Hosting ■ ▲
- **[creator]** A long-term host must (p. 17):
  - keep the artifact **reliably available indefinitely**
  - store **large volumes** of data
  - be **affordable**, ideally free
- **[creator]** Creators don't know of free services that guarantee long-term access. Large
  datasets don't fit into a GitHub repository or Dropbox. Google Drive, GitHub and Dropbox
  are **not long-term archives** and are ill-suited to large datasets (p. 17).
- **[user]** Being **unable to access** an artifact is the second most common user
  challenge (81 in Table 5; the text says 82) (p. 20).
- **[user]** In the publication survey, **~14 % of artifacts were inaccessible** via the URL
  in the paper. Older artifacts are more often unavailable (26.47 % for 2014), but even
  recent ones are affected (5.43 % for 2018) (Fig. 3, p. 20).

### C8 Double-blind review ■
- **[creator]** Double-blind review adds two difficulties (p. 17):
  - artifacts must be **anonymised** so that they don't reveal the authors
  - it is harder to find a host that allows **anonymous** sharing

### C9 External constraints ■ ▲ ★
- **[creator]** Some reasons are beyond the creator's control (p. 17):
  - **ethical or legal:** intellectual property restrictions, privacy
  - **technical:** the artifact is part of a larger ecosystem
- **[creator]** Industry collaborations: IP and contracts forbid sharing, and partners fear
  **liability**, e.g. lawsuits if data is misused even in anonymised form (p. 17).
- **[creator]** One author was criticised for not sharing data from an industry-track paper
  with practitioner co-authors: "Artifact should not be used as ritualistic blanket argument
  to judge papers" (pp. 17–18).
- **[creator]** **Privacy:** de-identifying personal data is error-prone and slow, and
  studies show that qualitative data can be **re-identified** even after identifiers are
  removed (p. 18).
- **[user]** **Licensing** makes it hard to build on and extend artifacts (p. 20):
  - source recovered with a **decompiler**, but the extended artifact couldn't be shared
  - some artifacts need a **paid commercial licence** for auxiliary tools
- **[reviewer]** Reviewers may lack the **licences** needed to install and use an artifact.
  There is no clear pathway when an artifact requires a **proprietary component**
  (Windows, a commercial IDE), and legal clarity would help (p. 23).

### C10 Lack of reviewer incentives ★
- Technical obstacles and artifacts unsuitable for sharing (e.g. undocumented) need
  significant time and effort from reviewers. This is **not recognised or rewarded** like
  paper reviewing (p. 23).
- "We usually just make **students** do AEC." This involves students in reviewing, but
  reinforces the idea that artifacts are not a primary thing (p. 23).
- Lack of incentives and recognition may lead to **inconsistent, low-quality reviews**
  (p. 23).

### C11 Technical obstacles ★
- Reviewing is complicated by **long execution times** and a **lack of compute resources**.
  It is unclear what to do with artifacts that need more than the time available per
  artifact (~ one day) (p. 23).
- Artifacts needing a lot of **disk space**, such as VM images, cause problems (p. 23).
- Some experiments are **practically infeasible to replicate** with an artifact, because
  reviewers can't run them: cloud-based work, fuzzers and automated program repair need
  significant hardware (pp. 23–24).

### C12 Limited communication ★
- Reviewing challenges are made worse by the **lack of an open communication channel**
  between creators and reviewers (p. 24).
- Some AECs use a single review round. Building an artifact takes time, and a minor glitch
  in the installation instructions shouldn't be grounds for rejection (p. 24).
- What matters is that artifact owners are **willing to help** resolve issues (p. 24).
- Even where communication is allowed, problems persist with **unresponsive authors**:
  without a defined protocol, reviewers may wait days without knowing whether a fix will
  come (p. 24).
