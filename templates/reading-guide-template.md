# Reading Guide (Template)

How to read a paper in limited time and come out with discussion points.
Based on Keshav, *How to Read a Paper* (three-pass method), and role-based
seminar formats (Raffel & Jacobson; presenter/advocate/critic).

## The three passes

| Pass | Time | What | Result |
|------|------|------|--------|
| 1. Survey | 10–15 min | Title, abstract, introduction, section headings, conclusion, figure/table captions, glance at references | Five Cs (below), the research questions, headline numbers |
| 2. Understand | most of the time | Structured read: claim → evidence → doubt (below). Skip proofs and fine detail | Filled notes table, doubts list |
| 3. Deep dive | optional | Mentally re-create the work; challenge every assumption | Only for the paper you present or the key method section |

**Five Cs:**
- **Category:** what kind of paper (empirical study, tool, position piece, …)?
- **Context:** what does it build on?
- **Correctness:** do the assumptions seem valid?
- **Contributions:** what is new?
- **Clarity:** is it well written?

## Pass 2: claim → evidence → doubt

Don't read front to back.
1. Write down what the paper **claims** (abstract, intro, research questions, conclusion).
2. Find the **evidence** for each claim (method, tables, figures).
3. Note where the evidence is **weaker than the claim**. These are your
   discussion points.

| Claim / RQ | Evidence (page, table, fig) | Doubt / question |
|------------|-----------------------------|------------------|
| | | |

**Margin codes:** `?` don't understand, `!` key result, `✗` disagree or weak,
`→` connects to lecture or other paper.

**For figures:** write one sentence on what you think it shows *before*
reading the text, then check.

## Time plan by paper type

### Empirical study (long, e.g. 25–35 pages, ~4 h)

| Block | Share | Section | Focus |
|-------|-------|---------|-------|
| Survey | ~6 % | Abstract, intro, conclusion, captions | Research questions, headline numbers |
| Background | ~10 % | Background / guidelines | Skim, 3–5 bullets |
| **Method** | **~25 %** | Methodology | **Read slowly**: sample, definitions, who classified, how validated, judgement calls |
| Break | ~4 % | | |
| Results | ~20 % | Evaluation / results | Map each result to a research question; check effect sizes |
| Discussion & threats | ~15 % | Discussion, threats to validity | Compare the authors' threats with your doubts. Tip: skim these sections already in the survey block, so you don't write critique the authors already address |
| Related work | ~4 % | Related work | Skim for links |
| **Synthesis** | **~15 %** | — | Fill in the discussion file |

If you fall behind, cut background and related work, **never** method or synthesis.

### Argument / position piece (short, e.g. 6–10 pages, ~1.5 h)

| Block | Time | Focus |
|-------|------|-------|
| Survey | 10 min | Central argument, headings, sidebars, conclusion |
| Read | 50 min | Follow the argument; mark which claims rest on **data** vs **opinion** |
| Synthesis | 30 min | Fill in the discussion file; compare with the other papers |

## Standard questions for empirical papers

- **Terminology:** which definitions does the paper use (2011 or 2020), and how do they map to the lecture's 2020 definitions?

- **Definitions:** how is the key concept measured? What counts and what doesn't?
- **Sample:** which venues/years/subjects? Does it generalise?
- **Timing:** when was it measured, and could that bias the trend?
- **Correlation vs causation:** does the text claim more than the statistics show?
  How large is the effect, not just "significant"?
- **Validation:** who classified the data, how was agreement checked, what
  error rate?
- **Reproducibility of the study itself:** are the data and scripts published?

## Discussion roles (need only the paper)

- **Presenter:** 3-minute summary.
- **Advocate:** why the paper is convincing and important.
- **Critic / reviewer:** weaknesses, accept or reject and why.
- **Practitioner:** what should researchers or the community do differently?
